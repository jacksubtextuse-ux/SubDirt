# Entitlement Pathway Taxonomy

Every legally available route to an approved project, with what each can deliver, who decides, and the
questions to answer before relying on it. Step 2 of the workflow enumerates candidate paths from this
list; paths are killed or kept **with citations**, never silently.

For each path considered, record: **mechanism + code citation · what it delivers (use / intensity /
both) · approving body · findings required · discretion level · hearing(s) · appeal exposure ·
typical duration (labeled) · disqualifiers checked**.

---

## 1. By-right / administrative approval
Use is a principal permitted use and the program fits the dimensional envelope; approval is staff-level
(zoning permit, administrative site plan).
- **Delivers:** use + intensity within the existing envelope. Fastest, lowest-risk path — but the
  claim "by-right" must survive scrutiny: quote the use-table entry, check every footnote, and check
  conditions embedded in the use permission (e.g., "dwelling units *in a mixed building*" is not
  by-right for a standalone residential building).
- **Watch for:** single-use building-size caps; ground-floor-use requirements; use permissions that
  apply only above/behind another use; definitional exposure on classification (see
  use-classification-guide.md).
- **Discretion:** nominally none, but administrative interpretations can still go against you —
  a zoning verification letter converts an interpretation risk into a written determination.

## 2. Site plan / development plan approval (commission-level)
Many codes permit the use but route projects of this scale through a planning-commission development
plan (sometimes styled "major site plan," "development plan," "design review").
- **Delivers:** project approval where use is already permitted; can also be the vehicle that *grants*
  the use where the code ties a use permission to plan approval (e.g., multifamily "per development
  plan" in a commercial district, TOD mechanisms).
- **Findings:** usually consistency/design standards — objective-ish but with real discretion at the
  margins (massing, buffers, access).
- **Watch for:** cross-reference ambiguity in the enabling provision (does "(o)(3)" mean an
  administrative plan, a PC plan, or a CUP? does it have a prerequisite designation?). If the
  mechanism's text is ambiguous, the memo says so and the pre-application meeting resolves it —
  never assume the favorable reading.
- **Political exposure:** a public commission hearing, but **no legislative council vote** — this is
  the structural advantage worth designing to when a rezoning would face a hostile council.

## 3. Conditional use / special use permit (CUP / SUP)
Use is allowed only on discretionary permit, typically from a board of adjustment/appeals or the
commission.
- **Delivers:** the use, subject to conditions the board attaches.
- **Findings:** the ordinance's CUP standards (compatibility, no substantial detriment, etc.) — quote
  them; they are the argument outline.
- **Watch for:** quasi-judicial posture (ex parte contact rules; evidence matters, petitions don't);
  conditions running with the permit; revocation/lapse provisions; whether the board can realistically
  say yes at institutional scale.
- **Appeal exposure:** typically to circuit/superior court on the record — cleaner than a council's
  political appeal surface, but conditions are where scope gets extracted.

## 4. Variance — dimensional only
Relief from a numeric standard (setback, height, lot coverage) on a hardship showing.
- **Never a use path:** most jurisdictions **prohibit use variances** by ordinance or state law —
  check and cite the prohibition early, because it kills the "just get a variance" shortcut and forces
  the rezoning conversation. Where use variances exist at all, hardship standards make an
  institutional-scale PBSH grant unrealistic; treat as non-viable absent extraordinary facts.
- **Legitimate role:** trimming one or two dimensional pinch points on an otherwise by-right or
  plan-approved design.

## 5. Zone map amendment (rezoning) — straight
Legislative change of the district to one that permits the use by right.
- **Delivers:** use + a new envelope; the clean fix for split-zoned assemblages (one district over the
  whole site) and the only fix where no current district mechanism reaches the use.
- **Findings:** state statute and/or ordinance — commonly plan conformance **or** changed
  circumstances. This is where Step 4's plan-conformance work becomes the case-in-chief.
- **Process:** commission hearing + recommendation → council adoption (readings, possible protest-
  petition supermajority triggers — check for one; a valid protest petition can raise the required
  council margin). Highest discretion, highest political exposure, longest tail.
- **Candidate-district selection:** pick the *least* intense district that delivers the program —
  overreaching districts hand opponents the "spot zoning / out of scale" argument. Document the
  candidate table (district, MF permitted?, dormitory permitted?, height, density control, fit).
- **Conditional/restricted rezoning:** many states allow rezoning subject to conditions or a binding
  concept plan — often the politically saleable version. Note availability and mechanics.

## 6. Planned development (PD / PUD)
Negotiated district: the ordinance's menu is replaced by a bespoke regulating plan approved
legislatively.
- **Delivers:** use + custom envelope + negotiated deviations — the tool when the program exceeds any
  existing district or needs standards relief too extensive for variances.
- **Cost:** longest timeline, most negotiation surface, public-benefit expectations (the
  zoning-compliance skill's `pd-strategy-guide.md` covers deviation framing — reuse it, don't
  duplicate it).
- **Watch for:** minimum site-size thresholds; required open-space/benefit ratios; whether PD is a
  rezoning (council) or a permit (commission) in this code.

## 7. Text amendment (ZOTA)
Changing the ordinance itself (add a use, create a mechanism, adjust a cap).
- Rarely a single-project path (slow, jurisdiction-wide, invites scrutiny), but pending ZOTAs cut both
  ways: a reform in progress (e.g., corridor-density enabling) can create a better path — tracked as
  **pending, never relied on** — and a hostile one (moratorium, occupancy restriction) is a risk to
  flag with its adoption timeline.

## 8. Development agreement
Contract with the jurisdiction vesting rights/locking standards, usually alongside a rezoning or PD.
- **Use:** schedule certainty, fee locks, phasing, vesting against pending code changes. Check state
  enabling authority and which body approves.

## 9. Plat / consolidation / assemblage mechanics
Lot consolidation or replat for multi-parcel assemblages; vacation of internal lot lines, alleys, or
easements; cross-access agreements.
- Not glamorous, but they **gate the schedule** and belong on the critical path (often runnable
  concurrently). Split ownership adds closing-sequence dependencies — flag when parcels are held by
  different owners (an operating-tenant parcel is both a title and a timing item).

## 10. Ancillary discretionary triggers
Approvals that ride along and can each add a hearing or a clock:
- Historic / design review (overlay commissions) — check overlay maps early; historic-resource
  demolition is a documented project-killer in contested markets.
- Traffic study thresholds and off-site improvement requirements.
- Stormwater / geologic-hazard-area approvals where mapped conditions (karst, floodplain) trigger them.
- State/regional permits (DOT access on state routes, environmental).
- Abandonment/vacation of right-of-way if the site plan needs it.

## Path-ranking discipline

Rank surviving paths on four axes and show the table in the memo:
1. **Certainty** — how much discretion stands between application and approval, and who holds it.
2. **Scope delivered** — does the path carry the full program, or a reduced one?
3. **Time** — critical-path months, base and contested (labeled typical vs. statutory).
4. **Political exposure** — number and type of public votes required, scored against Step 5 precedent.

The recommended path is not always the fastest — it is the one whose worst case the deal survives.
State the fallback path and the specific trigger for switching to it (e.g., "if pre-application
confirms the development-plan mechanism requires a TOD designation this site lacks, pivot to the
R-5 rezoning and re-underwrite entitlement at 18–24 months").
